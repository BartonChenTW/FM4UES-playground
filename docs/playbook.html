Playbook

Using foundation models in your own energy project

The four modules each made key lessons clear the hard way. This page collects them: how to pick a model type for an energy task, and a checklist to run before you trust its output in a planning study or a paper.

Which kind of FM for which energy task?

Your data looks like…Typical energy tasksFM typeTry first
Time series: load, PV, wind, prices, temperatures Short-term forecasting, gap filling, scenario bands Time-series FM Chronos-Bolt (univariate), Chronos-2 (with covariates). See Module 1
A table: one row per building, feeder or household EUI or demand of unmetered buildings, attribute imputation, classification Tabular FM TabPFN, if under ~10k rows. See Module 2
Images: satellite, aerial, street view Land use, roof and building detection, siting Vision / multimodal encoder Frozen encoder + linear probe. Prefer remote-sensing-trained models over CLIP. See Module 3
Daily profiles you want to group or generate Typical days, archetypes, anomaly detection, synthetic profiles Encoder / autoencoder embeddings Small autoencoder, or embeddings from a time-series FM. See Module 3, Part A
Power grid graph: buses, lines, generators, contingencies AC Power Flow, AC-OPF, N-1 / N-k screening, warm starts Physics-aware graph FM GENCO (GridFM) batched >= 32, followed by an AC feasibility check. See Module 4

Rule of thumb: start with zero-shot or in-context use. Move to a frozen encoder + small model when you have some labels. Consider fine-tuning only when both fall short and you have enough data to validate it.

Checklist before you trust an FM

  • It beats a naive baseline. "Same as yesterday", "always the average", "same as last week". Chronos cut the error by ~40% against these. If your FM does not, something is wrong. (Module 1)
  • It was scored over many cases, not one plot. A whole year of daily forecasts, or cross-validation over all buildings. Look at the breakdown too: all models failed in August. (Modules 1, 2)
  • No input leaks the answer. Every feature must be available at prediction time, for the buildings you will actually predict. ENERGY STAR scores and GHG intensity are computed from the target. (Module 2)
  • Future inputs are realistic. Measured weather used as a "forecast" is perfect foresight. Report which you used, and expect less gain with real forecasts. (Module 1)
  • Uncertainty is calibrated. An "80% interval" should contain the truth about 80% of the time. Measure it before feeding quantiles into a stochastic model. (Module 1)
  • Your data looks like its training data. FMs fail outside their domain, often fluently: BLIP described a residential area as "a satellite image of the moon". Check a sample of outputs by hand. (Module 3)
  • The features carry the signal. If R² is 0.3 for every model, the problem is the data, not the model. (Module 2)
  • Prompts and settings were not cherry-picked on the test set. CLIP accuracy moved 8 points with prompt wording. Choose settings on a validation split. (Module 3)
  • Runtime, memory and energy are reported. Each notebook runs fm4ues.profiler (50 ms NVML GPU telemetry + offline CodeCarbon) and exports structured records to benchmarks/results/. A GPU made Module 1 forecasting ~7× faster, and half-precision fp16 cut energy 2.5×. (All)
  • The licence fits your use. TabPFN v2 is open; TabPFN 2.5+ is non-commercial. Check model and data licences before using them in consultancy work. (Module 2)
  • Fast ≠ certified feasible. Neural grid surrogates can enforce box bounds (Vm, Pg = 0% violations) yet still emit nonzero Kirchhoff mismatch (204.9 MW max on 118-bus OPF) or minor thermal overloads (0.84%). Use them to screen or warm-start, not replace an AC verifier. (Module 4)

Energy footprint & energy ROI

Running all eight notebooks across the four modules consumes 0.71–6.28 Wh of measured GPU electricity per notebook (23.95 Wh NVML GPU total) and 2.11–39.47 Wh including CodeCarbon's modeled CPU + RAM system estimate (89.95 Wh total — less than running a 100 W lightbulb for one hour). For energy system modellers, what matters is understanding what each number measures and how deployment choices scale:

  • Measured vs. modeled vs. wall-socket. NVML reads actual GPU power at 50 ms resolution, whereas on Windows CodeCarbon models CPU via TDP × load and RAM via a capacity heuristic (~3 W per 8 GB installed — 42 W on a 617 GB server!). Neither includes PSU losses, cooling (PUE), or the upstream energy spent pretraining the foundation model once.
  • Where you run matters more than minor model tweaks. The exact same 2.27 Wh notebook emits 68 mg CO₂ on the Swiss grid (30 gCO₂/kWh), 521 mg on the EU average (230 gCO₂/kWh), and 861 mg on the US average (380 gCO₂/kWh) — over 12× variation from grid carbon intensity alone.
  • Three operational levers that save VRAM, time and Joules. (1) Half precision: fp16 cut Chronos-Bolt peak VRAM by 47% and GPU energy by 2.5× on Turing (use bf16 on Ampere+). (2) Batching: forecasting 365 days in one batch amortises ~25–55 W idle GPU power across hundreds of forecasts, and batching 32 grids in Module 4 cut GENCO 118-bus OPF from 71.0 ms (3.16 J/solve) to 3.4 ms (0.37 J/solve). (3) Frozen encoders: extracting CLIP embeddings once (0.50 J/img) and fitting a CPU linear probe beats zero-shot by 41 points without running an autoregressive decoder (5.95 J/caption).

Adapting a notebook to your own data

  1. Replace the loading cell with your data, in the same shape: a time-indexed series (Module 1), a feature table plus target (Module 2), a list of images (Module 3), or bus/gen/branch tables (Module 4).
  2. Keep the baselines and the evaluation cells unchanged. They are the part that tells you whether the FM is worth it.
  3. Run on a small subset first (a month, 100 buildings, 500 images, or 1 parquet partition) to check shapes and runtime, then scale up.
  4. Compare against what you use today, such as your existing regression, standard load profiles, or classical AC/DC solvers, not only against naive baselines.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Foundation modelA large model pretrained on broad data, then applied to many downstream tasks.
Zero-shotUsing the model on a new task with no training and no examples.
In-context learningGiving the model labelled examples as part of the input. The weights stay fixed (TabPFN).
EmbeddingA fixed-length vector that summarises an input. Similar inputs get nearby vectors.
Linear probeA simple linear model trained on frozen embeddings, to test or use what the encoder learned.
Fine-tuningContinuing to train a pretrained model on your own data.
CovariateAn extra input series that helps explain the target, such as temperature for demand.
Quantile forecastA forecast of a distribution's quantiles (e.g. 10/50/90%) instead of a single value.
Data leakageWhen an input contains information about the target that will not be available in real use.
